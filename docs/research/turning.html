Turning research for S6

2026-11-08 · for Morgan · what a lathe can do, so the app judges turned parts sensibly

The answer: the turning rules in the spec are in the right range. A part held only in a chuck is safe to about 3× its diameter long; with a tailstock to about 6–10×; with a steady rest to about 12–20×; past about 10× most shops would use a Swiss-type lathe. The spec warns at 4× and flags high at 8×, which sits inside those ranges.

Holes off the turning axis need live tooling or a mill, as the spec says. The research suggests 5 changes (listed at the bottom as proposals). I am building S6 as the spec says now and will ask you about each change one at a time.

  • One shop's limits: no tailstock 3× (parts up to 3 in across), with tailstock 6× up to 1 in and 8× from 1 to 3 in across. Protocase work envelope (table "L/D #1 (No Tailstock)", "L/D #2 (With Tailstock)").
  • Material matters: 3× unsupported for steel and harder; 5× "only for aluminum and soft materials", light finishing cuts. Lathe Mastery wikibook. This is the only material-specific figure found.
  • "Most manufacturers recommend that you use a tailstock to support any piece that exceeds a 3:1 length-to-diameter ratio." Absolute Machine
  • Swiss-type lathes: "Ratios above 3:1 favor Swiss; ratios above 10:1 practically require it"; most take bar up to 20–32 mm. Wevolver. MMS puts it at 4:1 or more. MMS
  • A commercial DFM checker (CAD Exchanger) flags a turned part over 8× by default, using the part's smallest diameter. CAD Exchanger docs ("Default value for this ratio is 8.")

Holding the part

  • A collet holds round stock true to about 0.0005 in; bored soft jaws repeat to 0.0006–0.0012 in; collets suit parts about 3 in across or less (5C about 1 in). CNCCookbook lathe workholding
  • Three-jaw chucks are quickest for round bar; four-jaw chucks also hold square bar and can be dialled true. eMachineShop, CNCCookbook.
  • Between centres gives very high repeatability when a part comes off and goes back on. CNCCookbook.
  • A tailstock needs a centre hole on the part's end: "a small dimple or a through-hole with a countersink". Protocase

Turning the part round (second operations)

  • Taking a part out of a 3-jaw chuck and putting it back loses concentricity; a 4-jaw can be realigned. CNCCookbook.
  • A sub-spindle picks the part off at cut-off and finishes the back side in the same cycle. Production Machining. Parting stops about 1 mm before centre for the hand-off. Sandvik Coromant
  • Bar stock allowance for cut-off and the bar end: 5% extra for parts under 2 in long, up to 10% for parts 4 in and longer. Production Machining (PMPA)
  • Putting internal features and threads on one end lets everything be cut before cut-off. Wevolver

Operations: boring, grooving, threading, drilling

  • Boring bar reach (turning, as a multiple of the bar's diameter): steel bar 4×, carbide 6×, damped steel 10×, damped carbide 14×. Sandvik turning guide ("maximum recommended overhang"). Vibration "will start to become an issue" from 4×. Sandvik long overhangs
  • Holes on the axis: 1 mm and up, at most 6× diameter deep. Protolabs. This matches the app's hole-depth warning at 6×.
  • Grooves: outside grooves at least 1.2 mm wide (0.5 mm in aluminium and brass); Protolabs does not do inside grooves at all. Protolabs turning guidelines
  • Thread relief: at least 1.5 threads long and at least 0.015 in below the minor diameter. Undercut (turning). Limit thread length to 3× the hole diameter. Hubs
  • Thin walls: under 0.5 mm "typically do not survive". Protolabs turning guidelines.

Features off the turning axis

  • A lathe with live tools can mill shapes and drill off-axis or radial holes. Protolabs. Without live tools the part goes to a mill as a second operation. MFG Robots
  • Radial holes 2 mm and up (1 mm in aluminium and brass); holes at odd angles only in soft metals. Protolabs turning guidelines.
  • True flats, off-centre holes and straight-walled keyways need a Y axis; with only C and X a flat comes out "slightly concave or slightly convex". MMS

Tools, corners and cost

  • Turrets: 12-station turrets are common on Haas lathes; one model has 24 (12 + 12). Redline Tools
  • Inside corners: "Sharp inside corners on a part will be radiused" (by the insert's nose). Protolabs turning guidelines. One Protolabs page names a 0.016 in finishing nose radius. Protolabs
  • Machining time is the main cost driver; aim for one setup. Hubs. Material, tight tolerance (±0.01 mm) and fine finish raise cost (one vendor's figures: carbon steel 1.3×, stainless 1.6–2.0× aluminium). MFG Robots.
  • Deburring is manual and adds cost to every part; avoid unsupported small-diameter work. Wevolver.

Not confirmed on an opened page, so not used: standard insert nose radii (0.4 and 0.8 mm), grooving insert widths, the smallest boring bar, thread relief width per DIN 76. Pages that refused to load: Xometry, Hubs design guide, Haas, GlobalSpec, Canadian Metalworking.

Proposals (all 5 approved and built: D-025, D-026)

  • 1. Slender limits by material. Spec: 4× medium, 8× high for every material. Research: 3× for steel, 5× for aluminium without support; tailstock 6–10×. Option: steel and stainless 3× / 8×, aluminium and plastic 4× / 8×. Decided 2026-11-08: this option (D-025), built.
  • 2. Say which support the part needs. Approved, built (D-026). The finding would name chuck, tailstock, steady rest or Swiss-type from the ratio (CADEM tiers).
  • 3. A boring-bar reach check. Approved, built (D-026). A bore deeper than 4× its diameter needs a carbide bar; over 6× a damped bar (Sandvik). The spec now uses the hole-depth limits (6× / 10×) for bores.
  • 4. Inside grooves. Approved, built as a note (D-026). Protolabs does not cut them; a note or a low finding could say so.
  • 5. Smallest diameter too. Approved, built (D-026). The spec divides by the largest diameter; a thin section behind a big head reads as stocky. CAD Exchanger uses the smallest diameter.

The 40% "no turning axis" share has no source either way; it stays an assumption.
